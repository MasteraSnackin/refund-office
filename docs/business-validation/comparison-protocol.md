# Manual versus assisted review

## Question

Does the prepared recommendation help a reviewer reach a supported decision more quickly without increasing incorrect amounts, missed evidence conflicts or unnecessary approvals?

This is a small exploratory study, not a claim of representative customer benefit. Synthetic cases test a narrow workflow; they cannot establish fraud detection or evidence authenticity.

## Prepare

Use the unchanged focused-12 pack in a fresh, offline rehearsal. Let its investigations finish and verify that the case numbers match the manual packets. Do not add clarifications, approve refunds or reset the run during a participant's tasks. Record the app version/source digest and evaluation report used. A previous completed refund or modified case is not an equivalent comparison.

Explain the outcome labels: “ready for human review” is a supported recommendation, “hold” means more evidence or a policy decision is needed, and “existing remedy” means the supplied historical record already covers the claim. None of these worksheet choices authorises a payment. For held cases, record any proposed amount that should remain on hold; a policy-limit hold can still have an established item value. For existing remedies, record the historical amount, not a new payment amount.

Give one unrelated practice example first, with the same help for each participant. Do not reveal the answer key or scenario names during measured tasks.

## Allocate tasks

Use group A for the first participant, B for the next, then alternate. Group A receives manual cases at odd positions and assisted cases at even positions. Group B receives the opposite assignment and works in reverse order. Across two participants each case appears in both conditions. Each participant sees each case once, reducing simple answer recall. This balances some order and case effects; it does not remove differences in participants, learning or case difficulty.

For manual tasks, supply only the matching manual packet. For assisted tasks, open the matching investigated case in the application and let the participant use evidence, policy checks, history and the decision brief. Both conditions use the same underlying fictional records and policy. Do not open the answer key until tasks are recorded.

## Measure consistently

Start the timer when all task material is visible and the participant begins reviewing. Stop when they have recorded an outcome, amount and explanation they are ready to defend. Exclude page-loading preparation before timing in both conditions. Record interruptions explicitly rather than silently removing them. Use “unfinished” if they stop; do not count it as a successful quick task.

Record the participant code, group, task order, case, condition, elapsed seconds, outcome, amount in integer pence, evidence used, requested information, confidence and any help. A hold with a clear reason can be correct. The goal is not to maximise approved refunds.

## Assess and report

After the exercise, compare the recorded outcome and amount with the facilitator answer key. Check evidence and rationale manually. Record corrections without replacing the participant's original answer. Keep all completed and unfinished tasks, including cases that make the product look worse.

Report participant count, task counts and unfinished tasks by condition. Report median elapsed time for completed tasks alongside correct-outcome and correct-amount rates, interruptions and assistance. Inspect per-case results across groups before interpreting overall time differences. Do not treat the small number of observations as a statistically reliable product claim. Do not calculate percentage savings from zero or missing baselines.

A useful next trial would use authorised real examples under the retailer's policy, with their reviewer identity, access controls and normal sources verified. That trial requires a separate agreement. This kit makes no customer contact and uploads nothing.
